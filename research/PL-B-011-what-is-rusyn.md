## The word

1. Rusyns are an East Slavic people of the Carpathians. They have also been called Carpatho-Rusyns, Ruthenians and Rusnaks. They never had a state. Most belonged to the Greek Catholic church, some to the Orthodox (`PL-S-138`, `PL-S-139`).
2. The Latin of the old records is *Rutheni*; the Hungarian is *orosz* or *rutén*; the Slovak is *Rusín* or *Rusnák*.
3. The same word was used for three different things, and the sources do not always say which:
   - **a people and its language**: the visitations of 1749 and 1772 write of a Ruthenian language and of Ruthenians as a people (`PL-S-141`);
   - **a church**: the Greek Catholic church was called the Ruthenian church, and in eastern Slovakia "Rusnak" came to mean a Greek Catholic, whatever he spoke (`PL-S-139`, `PL-S-141`, `PL-S-140`);
   - **a nationality a person declares** on a census form, from 1919 on (`PL-S-140`).
4. In Slovakia today Rusyn is a recognised national minority, separate from Ukrainian. The language was given a written standard on 27 January 1995 (`PL-S-140`).

## Where the three meanings come apart

They come apart along the southern edge of the Rusyn country, which is where these villages lie.

- In 1773 an official list gave each village its main language. For Zemplín county it has 109 of 139 Greek Catholic parishes as Rusyn-speaking, 21 as Slovak and 2 as Hungarian. Further south-west, in Abov and Borsod, no Greek Catholic parish was Rusyn-speaking any more (`PL-S-141`, p. 148).
- In 1806 the Mukachevo eparchy counted its people settlement by settlement and wrote down the language of the sermon: Rusyn in 1,276 settlements, Hungarian in 346, Romanian in 180, Slovak in 41, and two languages in about 110. Where the sermon was Slovak, the Greek Catholics were few, about 70 to a settlement and about 15 per cent of the village (`PL-S-144`).
- The census of 1880 was the first to ask for mother tongue. In the Prešov eparchy only about half of the Greek Catholics gave Rusyn (`PL-S-140`). These villages were in the Mukachevo eparchy, so that figure is context, not a count for them.
- A leading map of Rusyn settlement shows 1,455 villages that were mostly Rusyn about 1900, and says that over 200 more, mostly in today's south-eastern Slovakia and north-eastern Hungary, had Rusyn-speaking majorities in 1806 (`PL-S-143`). A historian of Rusyn emigrants describes the same belt, the districts of Michalovce and Sobrance among them, as villages once counted Rusyn that took a Slovak identity in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Their people called themselves Rusnaks, or "Slovjaks", or Slovaks of the Greek Catholic faith (`PL-S-142`).
- Rusyns counted in Slovakia: 97,014 by mother tongue in 1910; 95,359 by nationality in 1930; not counted at all from 1950 to 1980; 17,197 by nationality in 1991 and 33,482 in 2011 (`PL-S-140`).

So a family from this belt can be Rusyn by origin and by church, and Slovak on every form it ever filled in. Both can be true.

### Senné (Szenna)

A Roman Catholic parish village. Fényes in 1851 calls it Slovak, Rusyn and Hungarian, Slovak first, with 238 Greek Catholics among 845 people. Its Greek Catholics belonged to the Iňačovce parish. In 2021: 737 people, 548 Roman Catholics, 40 Greek Catholics, one Rusyn (`PL-S-147`). Marked Slovak on the 1910 map.

### Vysoká nad Uhom (Viszoka) and Čičarovce (Csicser)

Fényes calls Vysoká a Hungarian, Rusyn and Slovak village. The dialect survey of 1969 puts Vysoká among the same twenty "Sotak" villages (`PL-S-145`). Its Greek Catholics were in the parish of Čičarovce, which the 1910 map marks Hungarian.
